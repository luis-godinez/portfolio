Instrument Panel Keyboard

The Luna Keyboard and its Launch Pad numpad, which lock together along notched seams. I designed, engineered and manufactured both.

500+units shipped
UnderstandThe people

I started with my own wish list: a keyboard that felt luxurious, in a metal case, with design details personal to an aerospace engineer. I wanted the HHKB layout, a niche one that suits a Mac user, with Control where Caps Lock usually sits and no Control or Alt keys in the bottom corners.

When I shared the concept, the keyboard community wanted one too, and a one-off for myself grew into a production run.

DefineThe problem

A metal keyboard that felt like an instrument and suited how a Mac user types, made with my own CAD and a friend who could machine one-off parts.

PrototypeThe build

The HHKB layout frees both bottom corners, so a backlit moon takes the place of the usual right-hand menu and Control keys. The keyboard and the Launch Pad numpad join along notched seams inspired by spacecraft instrument panels, so they work apart or as one object.

Pen and pencil mock-ups came first, then parametric CAD with shared dimensions across the assembly.

The riskiest question wasn’t how it looked but how it felt to type on. Before machining anything, I 3D-printed a simple case, fitted it with real switches and keycaps, and tested thickness, typing angle and layout for comfort and wrist posture. That print was meant to be thrown away. It settled form and function before any metal was cut.

Machining then shaped the final form: no sharp internal corners, and tolerances set for the fit between the two halves.

The Launch Pad came second, made to match. The keyboard used an off-the-shelf circuit board. I laid out the Launch Pad’s own in KiCad, with my own firmware, and have since designed full keyboard boards too. My own board opened up options the keyboard couldn’t have: a 5×4 grid or a numpad layout, a slider, an optional rotary encoder in the top-right key, and an OLED showing custom graphics.

TestWith users

I kept sharing the design with the community as it developed, and their feedback shaped the layout and options before anything was final.

After a small first run, machined by my friend, I gave keyboards to a few well-known keyboard enthusiasts to build and judge on form and feel. Some featured it on YouTube and Instagram.

Their builds found what I had missed as a newcomer to the community. One builder found that a different brand of spacebar stabilizer interfered with the case, which I hadn’t accounted for. The bigger finding was the sound. Most keyboards mount their switches on a separate 1.5 mm plate of FR4, POM, brass, copper, carbon fiber or fiberglass, each with its own feel. Mine built the plate into the case at 4 mm, and that rigidity made it sound tinny and feel harsh, which drew pushback.

Back to PrototypeI refined the pockets in the plate to fit the common stabilizers and switches, milled prototypes by hand to validate the fix, then cleaned up the CAD for the production runs that followed. For the sound, I added gaskets between the top and bottom halves to damp the vibration. Gaskets are usually hidden; I let them show between the two layers as a design detail, in a choice of colors.
ShipThe outcome

The first batch was sold on pre-order and every unit was delivered within six months. Pre-orders that never arrive are common in the keyboard community, even from established makers, so every batch after that was built ahead and sold in stock, ready to ship. Buyers took no risk, and sales reached over $150k a year. In all, more than 300 keyboards and 200 Launch Pads shipped.

I then brought CNC machining in-house for tighter control of quality, precision and timelines.

What I’d do differently

Packaging. I did all of it myself: assembly, custom printed boxes, laser-cut foam inserts and boxing every unit, at about 30 minutes a keyboard. To keep costs down I treated my own time as free, and across hundreds of orders it wasn’t. Next time the manufacturer ships straight to a packaging partner, and I receive pallets of finished product ready to send.
